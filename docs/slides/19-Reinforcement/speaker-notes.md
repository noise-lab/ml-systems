# Lecture 19 — Speaker Notes (Reinforcement Learning for Computer Systems)

Per-slide brief, one row per slide in deck order. In-deck notes (the `::: {.notes}` blocks) are also visible in reveal.js speaker view (press **S**). Every URL below returned a page when the rows were written (arXiv abstract pages, GitHub repositories, a USENIX presentation page, the Sutton and Barto book page); papers without a verified page are cited by name only. The deck follows the book's reinforcement learning chapter in order: why systems problems are decisions, MDPs, bandits, value-based methods, policy gradient and actor-critic, then simulation and deployment, with a preview of Hands-On 19 (a four-server bandit, an ABR environment, tabular Q-learning, and discussion questions). | 3 | Decisions, Not Predictions | • **Figure:** the agent-environment loop; the agent (the policy) emits an action, the environment returns the next state and a reward.<br>• Say the three words that recur all hour: state (what the agent sees), action (what it controls), reward (the one scalar it is told).<br>• Contrast with the QoE classifier from earlier in the course: it was trained on labelled sessions and never changed them; an ABR agent changes the session by acting in it.<br>• The sum-of-rewards point: the greedy choice that maximises the next reward is usually not the policy that maximises the episode. |

| 5 | What Makes Systems Hard for RL | • **Figure:** five hazards as a strip: partial observation, exploration in production, a non-stationary environment, reward design, the sim-to-real gap.<br>• Each returns later: partial observability on the MDP slide (history goes into the state), exploration in the bandit and safety slides, non-stationarity and sim-to-real in the deployment section, reward design in the penalty experiment.<br>• The chapter's closing verdict, to keep in view while the algorithms get elegant: the systems that worked combined a learned component with a classical algorithm and a safety net. |
| 6 | Markov Decision Processes (section) | • Four slides: the tuple on a toy, the two value functions and the Bellman equation, the hands-on's ABR problem as an MDP, and the exploration problem every solver must handle. |
| 7 | The MDP | • **Figure:** a three-state video-player MDP (buffer low, mid, high); each arrow is *action: probability, reward*; green fetches a small chunk, orange a big one; −8 and −4 are stalls. Numbers are illustrative.<br>• Walk it: in "buffer low" the big chunk pays 1.9 when it arrives in time but stalls with probability 0.7; the small chunk is safe. In "buffer high" the big chunk is almost free. The best action depends on the state, and the value of an action includes where it leaves you.<br>• The chapter's networking tuples: states are measurements (RTT, queue lengths, utilisations), actions are knobs (window, weights, channel), T is stochastic because the network has other users.<br>• Define POMDP in one sentence, then say what every practical system does: the last k measurements go into the state, as Pensieve does with eight throughput samples. |
| 8 | Policies and Value Functions | • **Figure:** the Bellman backup tree: Q*(s, a) fans out to the possible next states weighted by T, each contributing its reward plus γ times the best next Q.<br>• Read the equation in words: the value of taking a in s is what you get now plus gamma times the best you can do from wherever you land, averaged over where you might land.<br>• The recursion is why the problem is solvable: one equation per state-action pair, with the same unknown Q on both sides.<br>• Preview the three families by how they attack it: estimate Q from experience (Q-learning, DQN), search over π directly (REINFORCE), or both (actor-critic). Expectation forms are on slide 36. |
| 9 | ABR as an MDP | • **Figure:** one chunk of the ABR loop: state → pick a bitrate → download time from the trace → buffer drains, stalls at zero, gains 4 s → reward → next state.<br>• This slide is a contract with the hands-on: the same state, action and reward appear in the notebook. Bitrates in the deck's simulator are 0.3, 0.75, 1.2, 1.85 Mbps; the notebook's ladder may differ slightly, the shape does not.<br>• The state is discretised so a table can hold it; that discretisation is a design decision the Pitfalls slide and the hands-on both poke at.<br>• The reward is Pensieve's linear QoE: 4.3 per second of stall says one second of rebuffering costs about the quality gap between the lowest and highest bitrate over two chunks. The weights are arbitrary, and the hands-on changes one of them.<br>• Baselines first, learner second: the learner has to beat buffer-based, a strong heuristic. |
| 10 | Exploration vs. Exploitation | • **Figure:** fraction of rounds the fastest of four servers is chosen, 200 runs averaged, from the hands-on's Part 1 setup (`bandit-best-arm.svg`).<br>• Pure greedy plateaus near 0.8: in about a fifth of the runs an early noisy sample made the fastest server look slow and greedy never revisited it.<br>• ε = 0.1 climbs to about 0.92 and would go higher with a decaying ε; ε = 0.3 spends three requests in ten on random servers forever, so it sits lower.<br>• In production, exploration costs user experience, which is why systems constrain it (slides 15 and 30). Chapter: "Exploration Strategies". |
| 11 | Multi-Armed Bandits (section) | • Bandits are RL without the transition function: actions do not change what comes next.<br>• Worth a section because many systems problems are bandits (server selection, A/B tests, configuration tuning) and because they isolate exploration. |
| 12 | Bandits: The One-State MDP | • **Figure:** the hands-on's bandit: a client estimating a mean per server, four servers with true mean latencies 120, 95, 80 and 105 ms (±25 ms noise), the best one unknown to the client.<br>• Define regret carefully, since the hands-on plots it: the gap between the best arm's mean and the chosen arm's mean, summed over rounds. The experimenter can compute it because the simulator knows the true means; the agent cannot.<br>• Linear regret means the same mistake at a constant rate (greedy stuck on the wrong server, or a fixed ε exploring forever); sublinear means mistakes become rarer.<br>• Chapter examples: a wireless access point choosing a channel; AuTO scheduling short flows by priority level. |
| 13 | Epsilon-Greedy and Regret | • **Figure:** cumulative regret over 1,000 requests, 200 runs averaged (`bandit-regret.svg`). Final regret: 3,018 ms for ε = 0, 2,665 ms for ε = 0.1, 6,231 ms for ε = 0.3.<br>• Ask why ε = 0.3 is worse than greedy even though it finds the best server: a fixed exploration rate is a fixed per-round tax, and over a long horizon the tax dominates.<br>• Ask why greedy is not even worse: in most runs it locks onto the best or second-best server; the average is pulled down by the runs where it did not. The hands-on asks students to look at single runs for this reason.<br>• Decaying ε is the pragmatic fix; the next slide is the principled one. |
| 14 | UCB and Thompson Sampling | • **Figure:** left, grey bars are the latency estimates and purple whiskers the UCB bonus; server D has five pulls, so its bonus lifts it above server C's better estimate and UCB picks it. Right, one Gaussian posterior per server; Thompson draws one sample from each and plays the max, so the wide server D curve is picked occasionally.<br>• Both are "optimism in the face of uncertainty": explore where it matters instead of uniformly at random; both have sublinear regret bounds.<br>• UCB1: Auer, Cesa-Bianchi, Fischer, "Finite-time Analysis of the Multiarmed Bandit Problem", *Machine Learning* 2002. Thompson, *Biometrika* 1933.<br>• The hands-on uses ε-greedy only; this slide is intuition for the student who asks what the better rule is. The chapter notes that in systems, where exploration degrades service, conservative or constrained variants are preferred. |
| 15 | Bandits in Systems | • **Figure:** a contextual bandit in Configanator's shape: client context in, one server configuration out, page-load time back, reward model updated.<br>• Contextual bandits sit between bandits and MDPs: the context matters, but the action does not change the next context, so there is still no planning. That is exactly configuration tuning.<br>• Configanator: Naseer et al., NSDI 2022 (CDN server settings: TLS, HTTP version, congestion control, chosen per client class). Mutant: Pappone et al., 2025, LinUCB over Cubic, BBR and Vegas (the chapter gives no venue).<br>• Constrained bandits, from the chapter: switching a configuration or routing rule has its own cost, so constrained formulations limit switch frequency or bound the chance of degraded service.<br>• A/B testing as a badly run two-armed bandit is a good one-liner: fixed split, no adaptation. |
| 16 | Value-Based Methods (section) | • Back to the full MDP: actions change the state. Learn Q from experience and read the policy off it.<br>• Four slides: the update rule, the hands-on's learner, the deep version, what goes wrong. |
| 17 | Q-Learning | • **Figure:** one table update worked with numbers: in a low-buffer state the agent fetched the 0.75 chunk, got 0.75 reward with no stall, landed in a mid-buffer state whose best cell is 6.2; target = 0.75 + 0.9 × 6.2 = 6.33; the old cell 3.4 moves a tenth of the way, to 3.69.<br>• Two things to stress. "Model-free": the agent never learns T or R, only Q. "Off-policy": the max in the target means the random exploratory actions do not corrupt the estimate, which is why ε-greedy and Q-learning go together.<br>• Convergence (Watkins and Dayan, "Q-learning", *Machine Learning* 1992) needs every pair visited forever and a decaying α; say that this is why the hands-on decays ε but keeps a floor. |
| 18 | Q-Learning on ABR | • **Figures:** left, the greedy policy's held-out QoE every 50 episodes with the three baselines dashed (`abr-learning-curve.svg`): final 53 against 48 (buffer-based), 33 (rate-based), 18 (fixed lowest). Right, one slice of the 96 × 4 table at mid throughput with the last chunk at 0.75 Mbps, greedy choice boxed (`abr-qtable.svg`).<br>• The curve is noisy early because ε is high and the table empty; it crosses the buffer-based line after roughly a thousand episodes and settles above it.<br>• Read the slice as a policy: empty buffer, lowest bitrate; the greedy choice climbs as the buffer grows. That is the buffer-based heuristic rediscovered, with the switch penalty folded in, so it hesitates to jump two levels at once.<br>• Students' numbers will differ (their traces are theirs); the shape should match. If it does not, the first suspects are ε decaying too fast and too few episodes. Settings here: α = 0.05, γ = 0.9, ε from 1 to 0.05 over 70% of 5,000 episodes. |
| 19 | From Tables to Networks: DQN | • **Figure:** the DQN loop: environment → Q-network → ε-greedy action; transitions stored in a replay buffer; random mini-batches scored against targets from a frozen target network; gradient step; periodic copy of θ to θ⁻.<br>• Same update rule, three engineering changes, each with a reason from the chapter: a network generalises, so one update moves many estimates; consecutive transitions are correlated, so train on random samples of the past; the target depends on the network being trained, so compute it with a frozen copy.<br>• **Read first:** Mnih et al., "Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning", *Nature* 2015; the earlier preprint is [arXiv:1312.5602](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5602).<br>• Double DQN (van Hasselt, Guez, Silver, AAAI 2016) decouples choosing the best next action from evaluating it. Networking: Orca (Abbasloo, Yen, Chao, SIGCOMM 2020), NeuroCuts (Liang et al., SIGCOMM 2019).<br>• The honest reason the hands-on is tabular: with 96 states a table is the better tool and students can read the policy. |
| 20 | Pitfalls | • **Figure:** the same learner with 3, 6 and 12 buffer buckets (`abr-discretization.svg`); six ends highest, twelve learns more slowly, three loses detail.<br>• Ask the room which curve ends highest before revealing; most guess finer is better.<br>• Twelve buckets ends lower because each cell is visited a quarter as often, so estimates are still noisy when ε has decayed; with four times the episodes it would catch up. Three buckets cannot tell "buffer is 8 s" from "buffer is 2 s".<br>• The general statement is bias versus variance, the same tradeoff as everywhere in the course. Overestimation and sample efficiency come from the chapter's DQN section and summary. |
| 21 | Policy Gradient and Actor-Critic (section) | • Value methods need a max over actions, which fails for continuous or huge action spaces, and give deterministic policies.<br>• Policy methods parameterise π directly and follow the gradient of expected reward. Four slides: the idea, actor-critic, Pensieve, continuous control. |
| 22 | Learning the Policy Directly | • **Figure:** REINFORCE as a loop: run the policy for a session, compute returns, raise the log-probability of actions followed by above-baseline returns and lower it for the others, step, repeat.<br>• Say the intuition before the symbols: you cannot backpropagate through the network you are controlling, but you can nudge the probability of each action you took in proportion to how well things went after it.<br>• Continuous actions (a sending rate) come for free by outputting a Gaussian. Variance is the problem: a session's return depends on many later random events, so a single lucky session misleads; the baseline is the fix, and a learned baseline is the next slide.<br>• Williams, "Simple statistical gradient-following algorithms for connectionist reinforcement learning", *Machine Learning* 1992; the theorem is Sutton, McAllester, Singh, Mansour, NIPS 1999 (statement on slide 36). |
| 23 | Actor-Critic | • **Figure:** environment, actor and critic; the critic receives (s, r, s′), computes the TD advantage, and feeds it to the actor as the weight on the policy gradient; the critic updates itself from its own TD error.<br>• The synthesis slide: value idea (critic) and policy idea (actor) in one system. The critic solves REINFORCE's two problems: it is the baseline, and it lets the agent judge an action after one transition instead of waiting for the session to end.<br>• A3C: sixteen agents on sixteen traces, gradients merged asynchronously; faster, and a form of domain randomisation. **Read first:** Mnih et al., ICML 2016, [arXiv:1602.01783](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01783).<br>• PPO's one-sentence version: never move the policy far in one update, which matters for a congestion controller that must not flip from timid to aggressive in one step. Schulman et al., 2017, [arXiv:1707.06347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347). |
| 24 | Pensieve: ABR with Actor-Critic | • **Figure:** Pensieve's pipeline: state (last eight throughputs and download times, next-chunk sizes per bitrate, buffer, chunks left, last bitrate) → 1-D convolutions and dense layers → actor softmax over six bitrates (critic used only in training) → chunk-level simulator on real throughput traces with sixteen parallel A3C agents → the actor alone deployed in the player.<br>• Three points. Pensieve learns from the reward alone, so changing the QoE definition retrains a different controller; the hands-on's penalty experiment is that in miniature. Training is in a chunk-level simulator on recorded traces (public broadband and cellular datasets plus synthetic ones). The headline numbers (12 to 25% higher average QoE than robustMPC) are from the paper's own evaluation; Puffer later found the sim-to-real gap for ABR is real (slide 28).<br>• **Read first:** Mao, Netravali, Alizadeh, "Neural Adaptive Video Streaming with Pensieve", SIGCOMM 2017; code and traces at [github.com/hongzimao/pensieve](https://github.com/hongzimao/pensieve). |
| 25 | DDPG and Continuous Control | • **Figure:** DDPG for a sending rate: the actor emits one number, exploration noise is added in training, the critic scores (state, rate), and the actor moves the way ∇ₐQ says.<br>• Two routes to a continuous action. Aurora's: a stochastic policy (PPO with a Gaussian over rate changes), trained in a simple simulator, with the paper's point that it generalises beyond its training range. **Read first:** Jay et al., ICML 2019, [arXiv:1810.03259](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03259).<br>• DDPG's: a deterministic actor and a Q-critic; the actor's gradient is the chain rule through the critic. Stability tricks are DQN's plus soft target blending; because the actor is deterministic, exploration must be injected as noise. Lillicrap et al., ICLR 2016, [arXiv:1509.02971](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.02971).<br>• Networking: AuTO's long-flow rates (Chen, Lin, Xu, SIGCOMM 2018), DRL-TE's routing weights (Xu et al., 2018). |
| 26 | Simulation, Training, and Deployment (section) | • The algorithms are the smaller half of the problem. Four slides: why simulation, what it leaves out, how the reward shapes the policy, how a deployed agent is kept from hurting anyone. |
| 27 | Train in Simulation, Deploy for Real | • **Figure:** measurements → simulator or emulator (trace replay, ns3-gym, Iroko, Park) → training → evaluation on held-out traces and emulated paths (Pantheon) → deployment, with new measurements feeding back.<br>• The cost argument: Pensieve trained on the equivalent of years of viewing in hours because the simulator replaces a real download with a division.<br>• Three environment kinds and their trades: purpose-built suites are clean and comparable but abstract; bridged simulators keep protocol models but are slow; trace replay is grounded but a trace recorded under one policy may not predict outcomes under another (CausalSim, Alomar et al., NSDI 2023, corrects that bias).<br>• **Read first:** Park, Mao et al., "Park: An Open Platform for Learning-Augmented Computer Systems", NeurIPS 2019; code at [github.com/park-project/park](https://github.com/park-project/park). Pantheon: Yan et al., USENIX ATC 2018. The held-out point is the one students need for the hands-on. |
| 28 | The Sim-to-Real Gap | • **Figure:** the simulator box (trace throughput, buffer arithmetic, one flow) against the real-network box (throughput that reacts to the agent, CDN, TCP, Wi-Fi, other flows, users who pause); the policy does well in one and no better than buffer-based in the other; the fixes loop back to the simulator.<br>• Puffer deserves a minute: a public streaming site run as a randomised controlled trial, tens of thousands of users over more than a year, algorithms assigned per session; schemes that beat BBA in simulation did not live, and a model trained in situ on the site's own data did. **Read first:** Yan et al., "Learning in situ: a randomized experiment in video streaming", NSDI 2020, [USENIX page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/yan); code at [github.com/StanfordSNR/puffer](https://github.com/StanfordSNR/puffer).<br>• The lesson for the hands-on's Part 4: the simulator is a hypothesis about the world, and the trace was recorded under some other ABR policy.<br>• Domain randomisation (Tobin et al., IROS 2017) is the engineering answer; in situ training is the scientific one. |
| 29 | Reward Design | • **Figure:** three learners identical except for the rebuffer penalty (1, 4.3, 20), evaluated on held-out traces (`abr-reward-penalty.svg`): average bitrate 0.97 → 0.92 → 0.90 Mbps; rebuffering 22.8 → 3.9 → 1.6 s per session; switches 43 → 21 → 26.<br>• Read left to right: bitrate barely moves, rebuffering collapses, switches shift because the switch penalty's relative weight changed.<br>• The reward is a proxy for the operator's goal, and the agent finds the cheapest way to score it (reward hacking: Amodei et al., "Concrete Problems in AI Safety", 2016). Ask what a penalty of zero produces (highest bitrate, constant stalls) and a thousand (lowest bitrate forever).<br>• Multi-objective rewards such as Aurora's are the norm because networking goals are never one number. This is the hands-on's Part 3 ending. |
| 30 | Safety and Guardrails | • **Figure:** a guardrail pipeline: the agent proposes, action bounds clip, a safety check passes the action or falls back to a classical policy, monitoring watches reward and drift and can retrain or force the fallback.<br>• The chapter's three approaches: constrained MDPs (Lagrangian penalties; CLARA for 5G slicing), hybrids (Orca over CUBIC, Sage's failure detection, Genet's curriculum of environments where the agent trails classical baselines), conservative exploration (action bounds; Dinh et al. 2024's control-barrier layer for SD-WAN load balancing).<br>• The chapter's verdict: hybrids are the most practical for now but each needs its fallback engineered by hand; formal safety with RL's performance is open.<br>• Reframe the fallback: not an admission of failure but the reason an operator would ever turn the agent on. Orca: Abbasloo, Yen, Chao, SIGCOMM 2020. Sage: Yen et al., SIGCOMM 2023. Genet: Xia et al., SIGCOMM 2022. |
